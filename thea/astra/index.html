Thea: Towards the Harness of Embodied Agents

Revisited after GPT-6 Astra

HAI Lab · Eastern Institute of Technology, Ningbo

Tools Harness What remains

Abstract. Thea carried the harness paradigm of coding agents into the physical world by adding a persistent readable world state and a verifiable judgment of each action. With GPT-6 Astra operating a robot SDK inside a coding environment, we revisit tool granularity and ask which harness components come from the model, the coding harness, or the physical-world layer.

01

Revisiting the Tools

Can a coding agent control a robot without a prewritten pick-and-place skill? We gave GPT-6 Astra in Codex the Astribot S1 Python SDK and its documentation, with no Thea component, and asked it to perform the four left-gripper tasks below.

The agent corrected early geometric errors and all four tasks reached the displayed final states: objects in the plate, a retrieved cube and closed drawer, and a loaded bowl delivered to the destination table. For these tasks, the model constructed control code rather than invoking a learned manipulation policy. The drawer video combines two sessions.

The agent registered head-camera RGB-D observations in the base frame, solved inverse kinematics against the SDK’s forward kinematics, screened joint samples for self-collision, and issued one motion segment at a time. Base movement used velocity pulses gated by lidar clearance.

02

Revisiting the Harness

Model · coding harness · physical-world layer

If the model can write robot-control code, what does a physical-world harness still need to provide? The original Thea addressed two gaps: making world state readable and action outcomes verifiable. We revisit those two gaps, alongside body constraints and reusable procedures. Codex supplies the execution loop and files, but these digital mechanisms alone do not guarantee the physical result.

World state and spatial memory

Does writing down rooms and routes help the agent navigate and revisit places? We compare Astra, Astra instructed to maintain scene-graph memory, and a navigation-evaluation condition: three runs per condition in each of two datasets, for 18 runs in total.

In HabitatGS/SuperNav, Astra completed 2 of 3 runs, instructed graph memory completed 3 of 3, and the navigation-evaluation condition completed 0 of 3. In LHPR-VLN, none of the nine runs completed. These small sets show a benefit in the first setting, not a solution to navigation in general. Labels retain each run’s original stopping criterion: reaching all stages without a valid completion/STOP is still not completion.

In graph-memory runs, room and route records were written to files and consulted later. The video, synchronized context stream and Workspace let you follow how new observations led to route choices and revisits. The graph is an estimate built within that run, not a supplied ground-truth map.

Outcome checking

Does a completed motion mean a successful grasp? No: in these Revo2 trials, the wrist reached its target while the cup stayed on its support. The distinction is between what the robot was commanded to do and what actually happened to the object.

In two cup-rim attempts, the wrist rose about 7.58 mm but the cup remained on the plate. The agent used post-action images to reject those attempts and revise its grasp. Observing the object exposed a failure that SDK completion alone missed. These were task-specific self-checks of unmeasured accuracy, not an independent evaluator.

Body limits and safety

Does installing a new hand also give the agent an accurate model of its body? Here it did not: after Revo2 was installed, the collision model still described the old parallel gripper. Even after calibration, changing the hand shape increased the landmark error.

Human-prompted checks compared observed hand-surface landmarks with calibrated model projections. At a fixed hand shape, local per-finger RMS errors were 1.35–2.20 mm; after slight closure, overall RMS was 5.26 mm, with a 9.57 mm maximum. A fit at one hand shape did not guarantee accuracy after motion, so calibration alone was not a safety guarantee.

Workspace

Skills and reusable files

What can the agent retain instead of rebuilding it for every task? Across the four S1 left-gripper tasks, capture, localization, inverse-kinematics and collision-screening modules were reused as files. This is reuse of procedures on the same robot, not evidence of transfer to a different embodiment.

The Workspaces attached to the navigation runs provide a separate, inspectable record: helpers and scripts preserve procedures, while scene_graph.json, where present, preserves discovered world state. The provided, modified and new labels distinguish the starting interface from files left by the run. These final snapshots document navigation files; they are not the robot-module reuse evidence described above.

Additional Revo2 trials

Did later attempts achieve more than wrist motion? These records show the same empty plastic cup progressing from failed lifts and a tilted grasp to an upright hold and one completed transfer. In that final transfer, the cup stood upright after release and hand withdrawal, with no in-trial manual reset. This is one demonstrated transfer, not a measured success rate; the full recording retains 13 cuts across unrecorded intervals.

03

What Changes and What Remains

What changed is who writes the control stack: the model can now generate SDK-level procedures inside a coding harness. What did not change is the need to observe the world again, distinguish command completion from task success, and keep geometry aligned with the actual body. These demonstrations motivate the responsibilities below; they do not establish general safety or autonomous self-improvement.

01

Tool granularity blurs downward

The four S1 tasks show the model writing below a pick-and-place interface. A harness therefore needs to expose more than one level of robot control.

02

The Embodiment Profile does more work

The Revo2 mismatch shows why the exposed body model must track the installed hardware, its configuration and its calibration—not just a nominal robot description.

03

Safety moves into the harness

SDK-level commands leave workspace bounds, collision checks and force limits to the surrounding system. Task completion alone does not verify that safety layer.

04

Evaluation precedes physical-world self-improvement

The failed cup lifts show why motion completion is not a task-success signal. Reliable physical-world learning needs outcome checks whose accuracy can itself be tested.
